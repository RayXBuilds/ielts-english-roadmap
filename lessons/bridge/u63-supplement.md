### Passage

**A** In the fictional mountain archive of Reth, a researcher compares drawings of a glacier made during different visits. One image places the ice beside a large rock, while another shows a gap between them. It is tempting to measure the apparent difference directly from the pictures. Before doing so, the researcher asks where each artist stood and which features were selected for emphasis. A glacier can appear wider or narrower when viewed from a different position. The drawings may contain evidence of change, but they also contain decisions about representation. The project begins by separating changes in the landscape from changes in the way the landscape was pictured.

**B** The first drawing includes a square shelter that still survives above the valley. A diary describes the artist sitting beside its eastern wall. The researcher visits that position and compares the visible alignment of three distant peaks with the drawing. This combination supports a proposed viewpoint more securely than a general resemblance between mountain outlines. A second drawing lacks any record of where it was made. The team leaves its viewpoint uncertain instead of choosing whichever location produces the most dramatic apparent retreat. The known and unknown positions receive different markings in the catalogue so that later calculations cannot quietly treat both as equally established.

**C** Season introduces another complication. A sketch dated early in spring shows a broad white margin that a later visitor labels snow. A summer view leaves the same area bare. The researcher cannot treat every white surface in a drawing as permanent glacier ice. She compares written descriptions with the visual marks and records the season attached to each source. The diary does not establish an exact melting date. It does identify a distinction the comparison needs to preserve. Images made under different temporary conditions may still be useful, provided the claims drawn from them match the features that can actually be distinguished.

**D** A surveyor's notebook offers a different kind of evidence. It gives a measured distance from a stone marker to the ice edge, together with a date and a description of the measuring line. The marker was later moved when a footpath was rebuilt. A council note records the relocation but gives no new coordinates. The earlier distance remains evidence about the original arrangement, while repeating the measurement from the present marker would create a different reference. The researcher therefore treats the marker's history as part of the measurement history. A durable object can seem to guarantee continuity even when the relationship that made it useful has changed.

**E** The archive also holds two published engravings that look like separate observations. A printer's letter reveals that both were derived from one artist's sketch. One engraving enlarges a crack to make the scene more striking. Counting the engravings as independent witnesses would therefore exaggerate the evidence, and comparing the enlarged crack with another picture could create an apparent physical change introduced by the printer. The researcher links both images to their shared source and notes the alteration. Their value includes evidence about how the mountain was presented to distant readers, even where their usefulness for measuring the ice is limited.

**F** The final catalogue gives each item a proposed viewpoint, season, reference features and source relationship, with unknown fields left open. It includes a transparent overlay showing which parts of two compatible images were compared. The overlay is accompanied by a note explaining why other images were excluded from that particular comparison. This makes the selection inspectable. Visitors can see a historical argument being assembled rather than merely receiving a dramatic pair of before-and-after pictures. The project offers a cautious account of particular observed changes and a clearer agenda for further research. Its precision comes partly from identifying where the evidence stops, including the unresolved position of the relocated marker and the viewpoint of the second artist.
