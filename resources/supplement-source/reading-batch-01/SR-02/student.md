# SR-02 · Should a Puzzle Have One Solution?

## Passage

**A** The fictional magazine Pattern publishes a puzzle that asks readers to arrange coloured pieces into a square. The editor expects one arrangement, but a reader sends a second arrangement that meets every printed rule. Some colleagues want to reject it because it differs from the answer prepared for publication. Others want to announce that all puzzle answers are equally valid. Neither response examines the actual problem. A solution earns acceptance by satisfying the relevant constraints. If the printed rules permit two arrangements, the editor needs to acknowledge both or explain a rule that was accidentally omitted. Personal expectation cannot silently become an additional condition after the reader has completed the task.

**B** A unique solution can be valuable when uniqueness is part of the promised challenge. It allows a solver to test whether all the clues together determine one result. But uniqueness is not a universal measure of intellectual quality. A puzzle with several solutions may ask readers to find all of them, compare their properties or discover what remains constant across different arrangements. Such tasks can demand careful reasoning. Their value depends on the question posed, including whether the solver is told to produce one example or an exhaustive collection. Finding a valid example and proving that no others exist are distinct achievements that should receive distinct descriptions.

**C** The editor's first repair adds a requirement that the red piece touch the top edge. This excludes the unexpected arrangement, but it also changes the puzzle. The magazine should state the correction clearly and preserve the original version for readers discussing earlier attempts. Quietly altering the online instructions would make some readers appear to have ignored a rule that they never saw. Version history matters because a judgement of correctness relates to the conditions actually supplied at the time. A later improvement can be worthwhile while still leaving the earlier reader's answer valid under the earlier rules. Recognising that fact is part of an accurate account of the mistake.

**D** An illustrator proposes making the preferred arrangement look more attractive in the instructions. Visual emphasis may guide attention, but it is a poor substitute for a rule when correctness depends on that rule. A decorative example can also be mistaken for a compulsory pattern. The magazine should distinguish illustrations of possible moves from statements of required outcomes. Readers should not need to infer hidden restrictions from the editor's taste. At the same time, an explicit instruction cannot anticipate every imaginable misunderstanding. The practical standard is whether the information needed to solve the stated problem is reasonably available and internally consistent, not whether language can eliminate every possible ambiguity forever.

**E** For testing, the magazine gives the revised puzzle to readers who have not seen the answer. It asks them to explain which rule excludes each rejected arrangement. This can reveal a missing instruction more effectively than simply asking whether the answer looks right. The editor also keeps records of valid alternative solutions. Those records help distinguish a genuine counterexample from an arrangement that breaks a condition. A surprising response deserves examination, but surprise alone does not make it correct. The same discipline applies to the expected answer, which must satisfy the rules even when its author has invested considerable effort in preparing it for print.

**F** The final publication accepts the second arrangement under the original rules and presents the corrected puzzle separately. It invites readers to compare the two tasks and identify exactly what the new condition changes. This turns an editorial error into a useful investigation without pretending that the error was planned. More broadly, good puzzle design connects a clear question with enough information to judge proposed answers. Sometimes that produces one solution and sometimes several. What matters is that the standards remain available to the solver and stable during evaluation. A puzzle can teach careful reasoning only if the people judging its solutions are willing to apply the same care to their own assumptions.

## Questions

1. An expected answer may be privileged even when another meets every published rule.

2. Uniqueness can be a worthwhile part of a specified challenge.

3. Having multiple solutions always makes a puzzle intellectually inferior.

4. Finding one example is equivalent to proving that no other examples exist.

5. An online correction should be disclosed to readers.

6. The illustrator should receive a higher salary than the editor.

7. Decorative emphasis can replace an essential rule.

8. Testing should examine how readers use rules to reject alternatives.

9. Unexpectedness alone proves an alternative answer correct.
