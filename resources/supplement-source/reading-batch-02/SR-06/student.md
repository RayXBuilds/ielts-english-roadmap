## Passage

**A** At the fictional Fen workshop, a class converts clean fabric scraps into a flat decorative sheet. The exercise uses a sequence designed for the classroom and is described here only as a fictional production process. Its purpose is to make dependencies between stages visible. Students often remember the action that changes an object's appearance most dramatically while forgetting a quiet preparation stage that makes the change possible. The workshop therefore asks them to record what each stage requires and what it produces. A successful reconstruction of the sequence must preserve those connections, including the checks that can send material back to an earlier step.

**B** The process begins when an arrival tray receives the scraps. An inspection card records whether each piece is clean and free of metal fastenings. Pieces failing either check are set aside and do not enter this classroom batch. Accepted pieces are sorted by thickness on a sorting mat. Colour is deliberately ignored at this stage because the class is investigating how thickness affects an even arrangement. The teacher explains that a different artistic project might organise the same materials by colour, but that would answer a different question. The order chosen for this exercise follows the property relevant to its stated comparison.

**C** Next, students use a cutting guide to make pieces of comparable width. They place the prepared strips in a mixing bowl, where the selected pieces are combined by hand. The bowl holds dry material only. Water is not added in this fictional process. The mixed strips are spread over a mesh screen that establishes the sheet's outline while allowing the supporting surface beneath to remain visible through gaps. Students compare the spread with a paper template placed beside the screen. The template identifies the intended boundary rather than becoming part of the final sheet. Pieces extending beyond that boundary are repositioned before the next stage begins.

**D** A pressure board is placed over the arranged strips. The class applies the specified classroom pressure while checking that the outline remains aligned with the template. The teacher does not provide a universal pressure value for other materials or equipment. In this exercise, the board holds the chosen arrangement while a demonstration binder sets. Students then remove the board and place the sheet on a drying rack. They use a condition tag to mark whether the surface remains damp. The tag records an observation rather than predicting an exact finishing time. The room's changing conditions mean that the schedule alone is insufficient evidence that every sheet is ready.

**E** When a sheet is dry, students examine its edge against the original outline. A weak or uneven section is recorded on a defect chart. A sheet requiring adjustment returns to the arrangement stage before being pressed again, while an acceptable sheet moves to a display folder. The return route is important because the procedure is not simply a straight line in which every object passes each stage once. The class records how often a sheet repeats a stage and why. This allows the teacher to distinguish extra work caused by an earlier arrangement problem from a delay caused by drying conditions, even when both sheets are finished at the same time.

**F** The final display includes one completed sheet and a diagram of its actual route through the workshop. Students compare that route with the planned sequence and explain any repetition. The task rewards accurate reconstruction rather than an attractive story in which every action went perfectly. A process description becomes more informative when it identifies conditions for moving forward as well as names of equipment. It also helps explain why two visually similar products may have different production histories. The sheet is evidence of an outcome, while the sequence record explains some of the actions and decisions required to obtain it. Keeping both allows a later class to investigate where a change might improve the process.
