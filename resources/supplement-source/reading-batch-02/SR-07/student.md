## Passage

**A** The fictional town of Orin owns a disused stone quarry. Its steep walls and uneven floor attract attention from residents who imagine several future uses. The council invites three advisers to discuss the site before selecting a project. Landscape researcher Mara wants the quarry's working history to remain legible. Event organiser Leon wants a place for gatherings that can be adapted over time. Ecologist Suri wants decisions to respond to observations of the habitats now present. Their proposals overlap in places, but their reasons differ. The council records who makes each claim so that a shared phrase such as careful development does not conceal disagreements about which evidence should guide the next step.

**B** Mara begins with the marks left by extraction. She argues that a few clearly explained cut surfaces can help visitors understand how stone was removed. Covering every wall with decorative planting would make that history harder to read. She supports a route that connects selected working features and gives visitors a way to follow the former movement of stone. However, she does not demand that every leftover object be preserved in place. Items with no documented connection to the quarry's work could be stored separately while their origin is investigated. Her priority is an intelligible account of labour, supported by evidence, rather than the indiscriminate retention of everything currently lying on the ground.

**C** Leon examines how the site might host small performances. He favours removable seating because attendance and programme types may change. A permanent stage would commit the town to one arrangement before it understands how people use the space. He also asks the council to calculate the time required to install and remove temporary equipment. Flexibility has a labour cost that a photograph of an empty venue would hide. Leon proposes testing one modest event before deciding on a regular calendar. He wants that test evaluated through access, setup and clearance as well as audience enjoyment, since a popular evening can still leave staff with an unmanageable routine.

**D** Suri asks for observations across more than one season before the entire floor is redesigned. A place appearing unused during one visit may support activity at another time. She proposes marking small areas for continued observation and comparing them with areas receiving limited intervention. This would help identify changes associated with the work while preserving information about ordinary variation. She opposes describing every plant in the quarry as valuable merely because it arrived without deliberate planting. Decisions should consider what is actually present and the purpose of conservation. For Suri, evidence about the site's current life matters more than a general preference for either untouched ground or a completely ordered landscape.

**E** The advisers disagree about the first public opening. Mara wants a guided visit that explains the surviving work marks. Leon prefers a short performance with limited equipment. Suri supports a small observation walk after an initial seasonal survey. The council does not merge these into a fictional unanimous recommendation. It records three alternatives and the information each would produce. This makes later choices easier to explain. A combined project might eventually be possible, but it would still need to account for the time, space and resources required by its different purposes. Agreement that the quarry deserves attention is not agreement about the next activity to schedule there.

**F** At the final meeting, each adviser states one condition for revising a proposal. Mara would reconsider a historical claim if new records showed that a supposed cutting mark came from later construction. Leon would reconsider the event plan if trial setup demanded more staff than the town could provide. Suri would reconsider an intervention if repeated observations showed an effect outside the intended area. The council keeps these conditions with the proposals rather than treating them as signs of indecision. They show how the advice connects with evidence that could change it. Readers of the meeting record can therefore distinguish both the advisers' current positions and the circumstances under which those positions might reasonably develop.
